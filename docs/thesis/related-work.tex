\section{RELATED WORK}\label{related-work}

Single-machine loss-grid optimization intersects loss-landscape
visualization, adaptive loss-grid sampling, heterogeneous
CPU/GPU scheduling, and AEOS-style autotune-and-cache patterns.

\subsection{Loss-landscape visualization and
analytics}\label{rw-losslandscape}

Our thesis takes the baseline from Li et
al.~\cite{li2018losslandscape}, who introduced a 2D
filter-normalized loss grid extending the 1D-interpolation
approach of Goodfellow et al.~\cite{goodfellow2015qualitative}.
The official implementation~\cite{goldstein_loss_landscape_repo}
uses MPI to distribute grid-point evaluation across multiple GPUs, on one or more nodes.
LossLens~\cite{xie2025losslens,losslens_repo} integrated this
construct into a multi-scale loss-landscape visual-analytics
workflow; comparing independently trained model instances is
one of its diagnostics. Within the reviewed literature we
found no evaluation of whether
\texttt{functional\_call}+\texttt{vmap} or
\texttt{torch.compile} transfer to the loss-grid pattern
(one model, many perturbations), or of how applicability
varies across architectures.

\subsection{Adaptive and progressive loss-grid
sampling}\label{rw-adaptive-sampling}

A different direction replaces the canonical full grid with
an adaptive sampler that evaluates loss only at points
predicted to carry visual information. A non-peer-reviewed
course report by Prakash et al.~\cite{prakash2024adaptive}
applies this to loss-landscape visualization, replacing
regular $\alpha,\beta$ sampling with an adaptive algorithm
focused on ``interesting'' regions. We considered this
direction but do not pursue it; our thesis keeps the grid
fixed and optimizes its computation, so adaptive sampling is
related work outside the evaluated mechanisms.

\subsection{Heterogeneous compute and ML inference
throughput}\label{rw-scheduling}

Task-parallel heterogeneous scheduling assigns
independent tasks across devices. In the simplest variant,
idle workers poll a shared queue and claim one task at a
time. For this primitive, chunk size trades load balance
against dispatch
overhead~\cite{kruskal1985selfscheduling}. More complex policies
include HEFT-style cost-model
scheduling~\cite{topcuoglu2002heft} and priority-based
work-stealing, exemplified by
StarPU~\cite{augonnet2011starpu}; these address load
imbalance and per-task cost variance.

An orthogonal approach splits a single computation across
devices, with per-device stages composing a pipeline. OpenVINO's HETERO execution mode is a
production example at the intersection of heterogeneous
compute and ML inference~\cite{openvino_hetero}: the
inference model is divided into stages, each stage is assigned
to a device, and the output of one stage is fed as input to
the next.

Adaptive request batching to meet a latency target is a
standard technique in ML inference
serving~\cite{crankshaw2017clipper}. OpenVINO's automatic
batching~\cite{openvino_auto_batching}
groups concurrent inference requests at runtime to
increase device utilization (on supported devices). At implementation level,
auto-batching operates on the input/request batch dimension
of one fixed compiled model: requests are packed into slices
of a larger input/output tensor and executed by a reshaped
batched model~\cite{openvino_auto_batching_src}. The cited
systems do not evaluate whether the CPU/GPU
inference-throughput ratio determines where heterogeneous
scheduling reduces loss-grid runtime; we adopt the ratio as a
working assumption, since a CPU with throughput negligible
against the GPU cannot reduce grid runtime under any
scheduling policy.

\subsection{Autotune-and-cache patterns}\label{rw-autotuning}

ATLAS~\cite{whaley1998atlas} introduces AEOS (Automated
Empirical Optimization of Software): empirically time
candidate implementations of BLAS inner loops (GEMM, DAXPY,
etc.) on the target machine, select the fastest variant for
the machine's cache and register hierarchy, and persist the
selection so it is reused without re-tuning. The same
empirical autotune-and-cache pattern appears in modern
deep-learning compilers: TVM's AutoTVM tunes tensor-operator
implementations with an ML cost model and caches the selected
schedule for reuse~\cite{chen2018tvm}.

In a shared-queue CPU/GPU scheduler for ML-inference
loss-grid, effective CPU throughput can vary with CPU worker
count and CPU batch size. Then the optimal policy is
per-(machine, workload), so a calibration step is needed
before policy reuse can amortize. We lift the ATLAS signature
from BLAS operations to (machine, workload) for this case.
Within the reviewed literature we found no evaluation of
whether the calibration step that shared-queue policy
selection requires amortizes across interactive checkpoint
sessions.

\subsection{Where our thesis sits}\label{rw-placement}

Prior work provides the core
algorithm~\cite{li2018losslandscape,goldstein_loss_landscape_repo}
and its interactive model-comparison use
case~\cite{xie2025losslens,losslens_repo}, the pure
self-scheduling
primitive~\cite{kruskal1985selfscheduling} and the broader
heterocompute-scheduler family~\cite{augonnet2011starpu}
from HPC/scientific workloads, heterocompute implemented as model-parallel and
request-batching in ML
inference~\cite{openvino_hetero,openvino_auto_batching},
the AEOS pattern~\cite{whaley1998atlas}, and the adjacent
adaptive-sampling direction~\cite{prakash2024adaptive}.
StarPU, OpenVINO, and ATLAS are design precedents for our
scheduler and calibration cache, not direct baselines we
benchmark against.

Available loss-grid
implementations~\cite{goldstein_loss_landscape_repo,xie2025losslens}
target distributed or multi-GPU scaling. The gap
is whether fixed-grid, perturbation-based loss-grid
computation can be optimized on one commodity machine with
one GPU, available CPU cores, finite RAM, and finite VRAM.
Our thesis studies single-device
evaluation, CPU/GPU scheduling, and the integration of
calibration reuse into interactive checkpoint sessions. Among
the cited systems, none directly targets this workload shape:
one model family, many perturbations of one checkpoint, shared
data, and fixed single-machine CPU, GPU, RAM, and VRAM limits.
