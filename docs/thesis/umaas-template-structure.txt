Abstract: although this section is the first one of your thesis, it must be the last section you write. This section summarizes the main aspects of your thesis and it should include: i) the description of the problem you tacked, ii) a brief description of the method(s) used, iii) the major findings or results, and iv) their interpretation and conclusions.
  Intuition: the reader decides from this paragraph alone whether to read further. Lead with the problem, end with the takeaway, and prefer concrete numbers over adjectives.

Introduction: the introduction must include the explanation of the problem you investigated, where the research in this report is placed in the existing literature and in terms of the state of the art, requiring a literature study. Furthermore, a problem statement is formulated and research questions are posed. Here you also sketch the structure of the report.
  Intuition: the literature-positioning subsection (often called "Related Work") contextualizes your thesis by situating it among existing literature. It proves you understand the current state-of-the-art and creates a foundation to demonstrate exactly how your research fills a specific gap or improves upon past approaches. Citations belong here when they define the state of the art, establish the research lineage, or make the gap visible. Do not use Related Work for methods-defense citations whose real job is to pre-empt reviewer concerns about an instrument, approximation, or measurement shortcut. For example, "real performance characterization exists, but our delay-injection tool is acceptable because it is a different category of artifact" is not a gap-building argument; it belongs in Methods scoping or in a footnote on the instrument.

Methods: this section must contain a wide description of your approach. If needed this can be spread over multiple sections.
  Intuition: methods are about reproducibility and instrument defensibility. Someone reading this section should be able to re-implement the load-bearing parts without further help and understand the scope under which the instrument is acceptable. Cite direct ancestors here when a primitive, variable, or rule is literally inherited by the method. Also cite work that scopes a methodological choice: if a simple tool is deliberately not a full performance-characterization framework, references to tools such as Accel-Sim should explain the category boundary and why the simplified instrument is valid for this thesis. The test is whether the citation changes how the method is interpreted or defended; if it only states the field's state of the art or the research gap, it belongs in Related Work.

Experiments: the description of what you want to find out and how and why you have designed your experiments are placed here.
  Intuition: state the claim each experiment tests and the verdict criterion (what would count as supporting, refuting, or being inconclusive about the claim) before reporting numbers. A well-designed experiment is one whose result is interpretable regardless of which way it falls — including the "baseline wins" outcome.

Results: in this section, you report the results of your experiments, including proper statistical analysis. Here the findings should be stated without bias or interpretation.
  Intuition: report all measured outcomes including regressions, baseline-wins, and inconclusive cases. Statistical bounds (confidence intervals, repeated-runs protocol) belong here; mechanism explanations belong in Discussion. If a number is suppressed because it is "embarrassing", the section is wrong.

Discussion: in this section, your interpretation of the results of your experiments are required. It is also important to place them in terms of what is known from the literature.
  Intuition: reconnect each finding to the literature anchor that motivated it — the cites that appeared in Methods and in the introduction's literature-positioning subsection should reappear here as comparison points. Surprises are the most valuable content; name them explicitly and propose mechanisms rather than papering over them. Platform-specific or deployment-specific observations that the canon left abstract (e.g. specific hardware tiers, OS, library versions) are introduced here.

Conclusions: you draw your conclusions from your research and answer your research questions in this section. Also, reflect on the societal implications.
  Intuition: answer each RQ in one paragraph against the verdict criterion you set in Experiments. Be honest about what was not established and what remains open — a well-bounded "we could not show X under conditions Y" is a stronger conclusion than an overreaching positive claim.

References: to develop your work, you generally make use of or get inspired by ideas, methodologies or software from others. You should always cite these sources in your report. This section is intended for it.
  Intuition: every cite should either appear in Methods (direct ancestor or methods-scoping reference doing load-bearing work) or in the introduction's literature-positioning subsection (state-of-the-art / gap-positioning reference). A cite that only appears once in passing prose is a hint that it does not pull weight and either needs to be promoted to one of these roles or removed.
